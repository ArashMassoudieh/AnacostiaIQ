Monitoring a bioretention stormwater system

John McCormack Rd NE · Washington, DC

A continuously instrumented green stormwater installation. Water level, soil moisture, and local weather are sampled on site, streamed to the cloud, and plotted here so the system's response to individual storms can be watched as it happens.

Both are WebAssembly applications that run entirely in your browser — the first load takes a few seconds while they download. The forecast is produced by a digital twin of the cell that pulls the current weather forecast for the site and re-runs the model each day.

The site

Bioretention cells are shallow, planted depressions that intercept street and roof runoff, hold it briefly, and let it infiltrate through an engineered soil profile instead of discharging straight to the storm sewer. Their performance is difficult to judge from a design document: what matters is how quickly a particular cell ponds during a real storm, how far the water rises, and how long it takes to drain afterwards.

This deployment answers those questions by measurement rather than inference. Sensors in the cell and in its standpipe record the water surface continuously, a probe in the soil records moisture, and the hourly forecast for the site is logged alongside them so every response can be read against the rainfall that caused it.

What is measured

Ponding depthin

Ultrasonic ranging above the cell surface, sampled every few seconds and reported as the average of ten consecutive samples.

Standpipe depthin

A second ultrasonic rangefinder reading the observation well, giving the water level within the media rather than above it.

Soil moisture%

A buried probe read once a minute through an analog-to-digital converter and scaled against dry-air and submerged calibration points.

Air temperature°C

Hourly forecast for the site, logged as a time series so it can be read against the sensor record.

Precipitationmm

Forecast rainfall depth by hour — the driver every other series is interpreted against.

Precipitation probability%

Also the control signal: it decides when the field station switches between its two sampling rates.

How the data gets here

A small computer in a weatherproof enclosure at the site polls each sensor on its own schedule, averages away single-sample noise, and posts every reading to a service running on AWS. It also pulls the hourly forecast for the site's coordinates and logs temperature, rainfall, and rain probability as time series of their own. The dashboard you reach from the button above asks that service for a date range and draws the result — it never talks to the field hardware directly.

Sampling that follows the weather

Most of the year a bioretention cell is doing nothing worth recording at a high rate, and the interesting behaviour is compressed into a few hours around each storm. The field station therefore watches the forecast: when no hour in the next day carries a meaningful chance of rain, it stretches every sensor interval by a factor of ten and idles. When rain reappears in the forecast, the base intervals are restored before the storm arrives.

The forecast poll itself is never slowed down, because it is the thing that notices rain returning. The station also distinguishes a confidently dry forecast from a missing one, and only idles on the former — a failed weather lookup keeps it sampling at full rate rather than sleeping through an event.

Part of a digital twin

Measurement is the first half of a larger effort. A bioretention cell is normally credited with the runoff reduction its design calls for, and checked afterwards by periodic visual inspection. Whether a particular cell is still infiltrating what it was sized for — or has quietly lost capacity as its media compacts, its planting changes, or sediment accumulates — is rarely known with any confidence. Direct flow monitoring would settle it, but retrofitting a weir or a calibrated outlet to assets that were never designed for it does not scale beyond a handful of research sites.

The alternative is to pair cheap, retrofittable sensors like the ones here with a process-based model of the cell, and keep the two in step. In that arrangement, called a digital twin, a simulation of the cell runs continuously: one loop advances it in real time on incoming weather to produce a current picture and a short forecast, while a second, slower loop reads the accumulating sensor record and adjusts the model's physical parameters — saturated hydraulic conductivity, storage capacity, outlet behaviour — so the model keeps explaining the data.

Because those parameters are physical properties rather than the internal weights of a statistical predictor, watching them drift is itself the measurement of interest: a conductivity estimate that falls steadily across a season is what clogging looks like from the outside. Recent observations are weighted more heavily than old ones, so the estimates track the cell as it actually changes instead of averaging its whole history together.

Status. Two things are running here. The data-collection layer — the sensors, the field station, and the record behind the charts — and a digital twin of the cell, which pulls the weather forecast for the site each day and simulates the coming week; that is what the forecast button above opens. The two are not yet joined: the twin runs on forecast weather alone, and the assimilation loop that would correct it against these sensor readings is still to be connected. The framework, OpenHydroTwin, is described in the preprint below, where assimilation is demonstrated on a synthetic bioretention case rather than on field data from this site.

Shakouri, B., Kiriazes, R., Massoudieh, A. OpenHydroTwin: A Generic Digital Twin Framework for Stormwater Infrastructure. Preprint submitted to Environmental Modelling & Software, 2026. Built on the open-source OpenHydroQual simulation engine.
